# The Architecture of Orientation

## Knowledge, meaning, agency, norms, correction, and power

### Abstract

Human beings do not first encounter a neutral world and subsequently add value to it. We inhabit worlds already structured by attention, embodiment, relationship, inherited norms, practical possibility, and ranked goods. We do not merely hold beliefs inside those worlds: we acquire identities, perceive affordances, undertake commitments, and become answerable to others across time.

The appropriate unit of analysis is therefore neither the isolated belief nor the isolated individual. It is the **person-in-a-field-over-time**: an embodied agent whose orientation is formed through encounter, culture, authority, action, consequence, and correction.

This architecture integrates several problems normally studied apart:

- tacit and embodied knowledge;
- attention and perception;
- telos, meaning, and identity;
- cultural norms and myth;
- action, sacrifice, and accountability;
- insight, transformation, and relapse;
- individual and collective agency;
- education, authority, and social formation;
- psychopathology and psychiatric classification;
- ideological rigidity, totalitarianism, and institutional power;
- the relationship between psychological structure and theological interpretation.

Its central problem is:

> How can a finite person or community become sufficiently stable to act, sufficiently open to encounter reality, and sufficiently corrigible to change without dissolving the commitments that make action possible?

The answer proposed here is not permanent openness or final closure. It is **corrigible orientation**: the capacity to act from a meaningful order, receive what that order excludes, undergo proportionate transformation, retain what has been learned, and preserve the conditions for future correction.

Orientation itself is descriptive. Corrigibility is a proposed quality of an orientation, not part of its definition. An orientation can be meaningful, durable, and powerful while remaining false, exploitative, or incapable of correction.

### Scope and architecture

A concept belongs to the core theory of orientation only if it determines:

- what appears to an agent;
- what matters;
- where the agent is situated;
- what the agent can do;
- how the orientation can change.

The manuscript therefore distinguishes five explanatory levels:

1. **Conditions:** reality, embodiment, relationship, history, and legibility.
2. **Orientation:** disclosure, guidance, location, and practical repertoire.
3. **Transformation:** action, consequence, correction, retention, and recursive agency.
4. **Collective extension:** norms, authority, institutions, myth, and power.
5. **Evaluation and interpretation:** warrant, moral invariance, Logos, and metaphysics.

Marriage, addiction, cultic control, totalitarianism, and psychiatry are applications of the architecture rather than additional components of it. Christian metaphysics is an interpretation of its deepest pattern rather than an empirical stage within it.

### Epistemic status of the claims

The document contains four kinds of claim that must not borrow authority from one another:

- **Definitions:** proposed distinctions intended to make the architecture coherent.
- **Empirical hypotheses:** claims that should survive operationalization, comparison, and possible failure.
- **Normative proposals:** claims about qualities an orientation ought to preserve.
- **Metaphysical interpretations:** accounts of why the recurring structure may exist at all.

Conceptual elegance does not establish an empirical hypothesis. Empirical recurrence does not establish a moral obligation. Psychological usefulness does not establish a metaphysics. Metaphysical interpretation may orient inquiry, but it cannot be used to insulate the lower-level claims from correction.

## 1. Reality exceeds every representation

The starting point is epistemic finitude.

Reality may be progressively known, but no representation exhausts it. Every perception selects. Every concept compresses. Every model excludes. Every institution renders some things legible while leaving others tacit, invisible, or officially unintelligible.

This does not imply relativism. A map is not the territory, but maps are not therefore equal. Reality resists them differently through:

- failed prediction;
- frustrated action;
- contradiction;
- suffering;
- testimony from affected persons;
- ecological and institutional breakdown;
- discrepancies between promises and consequences.

Consequences do not interpret themselves, but they constrain interpretation. The gun may really fire. The cure may repeatedly fail. The allegedly voluntary community may require surveillance to prevent departure. A legal fiction may become uninhabitable even while remaining officially valid.

Objectivity is therefore not possession of a view from nowhere. It is the disciplined attempt to discover what survives changes of perspective, medium, scale, and interest while remaining answerable to reality's resistance.

The guiding image is simple:

> The needle that points north is not north. Its difference from north is what allows it to orient us.

Representation is neither identical with reality nor severed from it. Its purpose is truthful orientation.

## 2. Two forms of legibility

Human knowledge is not exhausted by propositions.

An experienced mechanic may hear that an engine is wrong before identifying the relevant acoustic features. A musician may disclose longing, terror, suspension, or homecoming without translating them into declarative sentences. A ritual may train a person's relation to death, obligation, or community without first explaining a theory of those things.

This yields two forms of legibility.

### Symbolic legibility

Something can be:

- named;
- classified;
- recorded;
- measured;
- transmitted propositionally;
- inserted into an explicit model.

### Experiential legibility

Something becomes recognizable through being:

- heard;
- enacted;
- inhabited;
- suffered;
- practised;
- encountered in relationship;
- disclosed by a change in attention.

The distinction is not between clear knowledge and mystical obscurity. It is between different native modes of disclosure.

Interrogating tacit knowledge can destroy the conditions under which it operates. The expert's later explanation may be a useful reconstruction, but it is not necessarily a transparent transcription of the original judgement. Recognition can precede articulation, and articulation can alter recognition.

The right relation is therefore not extraction but encounter:

\[
\text{presence}
\rightarrow
\text{arrested attention}
\rightarrow
\text{being affected}
\rightarrow
\text{recognition}
\rightarrow
\text{possible articulation}
\]

Representation is not encounter. But representation can point toward, preserve, stage, evoke, or partially reenact an encounter. A cathedral does not define awe; it arranges material, light, sound, height, and bodily movement so that awe becomes available. A story does not define grief; it can bring a person through a temporal form in which grief becomes recognizable.

This is the epistemic domain of art, music, poetry, ritual, prayer, myth, architecture, pilgrimage, beauty, personality, love, illness, grief, and experiences of the sacred.

## 3. Invariants under transformation

If an encountered reality can appear in different media, cultures, and practices, its invariant structure may become visible through variation.

Homecoming, for example, can appear as:

- departure and tonal return in music;
- exile and return in narrative;
- separation and reintegration in ritual;
- an actual return to a changed home.

A candidate invariant is:

\[
\text{prior belonging}
\rightarrow
\text{separation}
\rightarrow
\text{continued orientation}
\rightarrow
\text{changed return}
\rightarrow
\text{recognition across difference}
\]

The invariant need not be a proposition. It may be a relation, movement, tension, proportion, sequence, or characteristic transformation.

The method is:

\[
\text{one apparent reality}
\rightarrow
\text{multiple transformations}
\rightarrow
\text{comparison of differences}
\rightarrow
\text{candidate invariant}
\]

This method applies to mythology, moral reasoning, music, social institutions, clinical change, and theology. It also provides a non-naive account of universality. A universal need not be an identical surface form. It may be a structure preserved across culturally necessary transformations.

## 4. The person-in-a-field-over-time

The fundamental psychological object is **orientation**:

> The organised relationship by which an embodied agent attends to a world, distinguishes value within it, acts toward possible futures, coordinates with others, and maintains an identity through change.

Orientation is a relation, not a bag of properties contained inside an isolated person. Its state can be represented as:

\[
O_t=(D_t,G_t,L_t,\Pi_t)
\]

where:

- \(D\): **disclosure**—what appears and how it appears through attention, attunement, affect, skill, and representation;
- \(G\): **guidance**—the ranked goods, teloi, and relevance structures by which possibilities matter;
- \(L\): **location**—identity, role, relationship, and temporal self-location;
- \(\Pi\): **practical repertoire**—the actions and policies that appear available from that location.

Orientation occurs within constitutive and environmental conditions:

\[
C_t=(W_t,B_t,R_t,N_t,H_t)
\]

where:

- \(W\): material world, affordances, constraints, and resistance;
- \(B\): embodiment, capacity, vulnerability, and physiological state;
- \(R\): concrete others and relationships;
- \(N\): norms, institutions, and authorities;
- \(H\): history, memory, development, and prior formation.

“Context” here does not mean an external container. Embodiment and relationship are partly constitutive of the agent rather than merely surrounding it. The division separates the current organization of orientation from conditions upon which that organization depends.

These divisions are analytical rather than causally independent. Affect changes disclosure; norms shape guidance; embodiment constrains practical repertoire; relationship partly constitutes identity; action alters both agent and context.

The word **disclosure** is intentionally broader than “model.” An expert may be accurately oriented through bodily readiness, trained discrimination, procedural skill, or affective sensitivity without possessing an explicit representation of the source of that orientation.

The unit is not a person placed inside a passive environment. It is a coupled system:

\[
\boxed{\text{person}\leftrightarrow\text{attentional world}\leftrightarrow\text{normative field}}
\]

It is also diachronic. Present action modifies the future person who will attend, value, and act. Institutions preserve prior actions as the environment of later agents. Orientation changes through an update process:

\[
O_{t+1}=U(O_t,E_t,X_t;C_t)
\]

where \(E\) is encounter, \(X\) is action and consequence, and \(U\) is the architecture of correction. Context also changes: action, collective enforcement, and institutional power alter the possibility landscape in which future orientation occurs.

## 5. Presence, telos, attention, and attunement

Objects do not possess one fixed practical meaning. An island can be:

- a destination;
- a dangerous reef;
- a landmark;
- evidence that home is near;
- irrelevant background.

Its significance depends on the agent's telos. Several terms that are often collapsed should be separated:

- **Value:** a dimension along which states can be better or worse.
- **Good:** something apprehended as worth realizing or preserving.
- **Telos:** an organizing end that orders subordinate goods.
- **Goal:** a bounded target within a practical sequence.
- **Policy:** a strategy for reaching a goal.
- **Action:** an actual intervention under concrete constraints.

The fuller practical sequence is:

\[
\text{love or belonging}
\rightarrow
\text{telos}
\rightarrow
\text{proximate goal}
\rightarrow
\text{orientation}
\rightarrow
\text{attention}
\rightarrow
\text{attunement}
\rightarrow
\text{judgement}
\rightarrow
\text{action}
\rightarrow
\text{feedback}
\]

Goals are nested. Avoiding a reef serves maintaining course; maintaining course serves reaching port; reaching port serves returning home; returning home serves renewed participation in a beloved community.

Attention and attunement must be distinguished.

- **Attention:** the wind is strong.
- **Attunement:** the wind is south-southeast; given this vessel, heading, sea state, and destination, a specific correction is required.

Attunement is:

\[
\text{perception}
+
\text{discrimination}
+
\text{situational significance}
+
\text{readiness to respond}
\]

Affect joins attention to value and action. It marks significance before full articulation, registers progress or obstruction relative to telos, changes attentional breadth, and prepares response. Anxiety, shame, attraction, grief, confidence, disgust, and awe are not merely outputs appended to cognition. They are modes in which the condition of agent–world coupling becomes available to the agent.

Disclosure can therefore be schematized as:

\[
D=\text{attention}+\text{attunement}+\text{affective salience}
\]

Telos can direct attention, but encounter can also transform telos. Beauty, another person, suffering, art, or God may arrest attention before the agent possesses a relevant goal:

\[
\text{unexpected presence}
\rightarrow
\text{attention}
\rightarrow
\text{emerging attunement}
\rightarrow
\text{recognition}
\rightarrow
\text{reorientation}
\]

This prevents the architecture from becoming a closed theory of instrumental goal pursuit. Agents do not only impose projects on reality. They can be addressed by what they did not seek.

## 6. Modes of disclosure: attention discloses worlds

The previous section distinguished the components of disclosure. This section concerns its contrasting modes. Attention does not merely choose among fully constituted neutral objects. A mode of attention partly determines the world that becomes experientially available.

Narrow, grasping attention discloses:

- bounded objects;
- explicit distinctions;
- repeatable categories;
- manipulable means;
- immediate targets;
- relatively decontextualized representations.

Broad, relational attention discloses:

- context;
- novelty;
- living wholes;
- implicit significance;
- relationship;
- possibilities not specified in advance.

Both are necessary. Broad attention encounters and discovers. Narrow attention differentiates and operationalizes. The result must then return to the whole from which it was abstracted.

The healthy movement is:

\[
\text{encountered whole}
\rightarrow
\text{differentiation}
\rightarrow
\text{usable representation}
\rightarrow
\text{reintegration}
\]

Pathology begins when representation forgets that it is representation: when the instrument of differentiation claims authority over the whole that commissioned it.

This is simultaneously an attentional error, an epistemic error, and a possible interpretation of idolatry.

## 7. Meaning, order, chaos, and identity

### Experienced meaning and warranted orientation

Experienced meaning should not be treated as a single scalar. A workable vector is:

\[
\mu=(C,P,M)
\]

where:

- \(C\): coherence—experience forms an intelligible whole;
- \(P\): purpose—present action serves valued possibility;
- \(M\): mattering—the agent participates in consequential relationship.

A fanatic can possess intense coherence, purpose, and mattering while suppressing reality-contact. Meaningfulness is psychologically necessary but morally insufficient.

The experienced meaningfulness of an orientation must therefore be distinguished from its quality or warrant:

\[
q=(\text{reality-responsiveness},\text{corrigibility},\text{reciprocity},\text{cross-scale viability})
\]

Meaning describes how inhabitable an orientation is experienced as being. Warrant concerns whether it remains answerable to reality, other persons, excluded costs, and consequences across time. Neither can simply replace the other: an accurate but uninhabitable orientation may not sustain agency, while a deeply meaningful but incorrigible orientation may become catastrophic.

### Order

Order is not mere regularity. It is successful agent-world coupling:

- events remain sufficiently intelligible;
- actions have sufficiently expected consequences;
- norms render others sufficiently predictable;
- identity supplies a continuous standpoint;
- the future contains actionable possibilities.

Order means:

> I know approximately where I am, who I am, what matters, who others are to me, and what can be done.

### Anomaly

An anomaly is something the current orientation cannot metabolize. Its severity depends not simply on magnitude but on depth of propagation.

A broken tool may challenge one policy. A betrayal may challenge a relationship, one's judgement, one's identity, the trustworthiness of others, and the moral order at once.

### Chaos

Chaos is recursive model failure: the agent no longer knows which part of the orientation can be trusted to repair the others.

This is why chaos is closely related to identity. Identity is partly the persistent standpoint from which error can be confronted. When that standpoint becomes the uncertain object, ordinary correction becomes impossible.

The death-and-rebirth pattern follows. Major accommodation may require the death of the identity whose world has become uninhabitable. Something persists, but its organization changes.

## 8. Competing goods, action, and sacrifice

People commonly affirm several goods while circumstances allow them to coexist. Crisis forces ranking because one action rather than another must occur.

\[
\text{plural goods}
\rightarrow
\text{concrete conflict}
\rightarrow
\text{ranking}
\rightarrow
\text{sacrifice}
\rightarrow
\text{action}
\]

Action makes a ranking operative, but it does not transparently reveal an eternal essence. A crisis discloses an event under severe constraints, not the person's complete or final character.

Description then becomes morally consequential. Statements such as “I escaped,” “I fled,” “I panicked,” “I went for help,” and “I abandoned her” can refer to similar bodily movements while selecting different teloi, obligations, causal structures, and distributions of responsibility.

Description is never merely decorative. It can:

- preserve morally relevant structure;
- obscure constraints;
- retroactively invent an obligation;
- erase relationship;
- turn one person's discovery into another person's defect;
- determine what becomes institutionally actionable.

The question is not whether interpretation can be eliminated. It is which description best preserves the event's physical, relational, intentional, temporal, and normative structure.

## 9. Accountability without total legibility

Persons remain partly illegible. Consequences do not.

Accountability connects action to relationship and consequence without claiming perfect access to character or motive.

It asks:

> Who is answerable to whom, for what, under which obligation, with what capacity, and requiring what response?

Accountability can require:

- an honest account;
- ownership of one's contribution;
- acknowledgement of another's experience;
- contesting a falsely attributed obligation;
- attempted repair;
- acceptance of consequences;
- development of future capacity;
- renegotiation of an altered relationship.

It is distinct from causal responsibility, blame, justification, punishment, and repair.

Judgements can diverge. A person may have been justified while another can no longer rely on them. Someone may have acted wrongly while the relationship survives. Relational continuation is not identical to acquittal, and dissolution is not identical to proof of fault.

An event may not reveal who a person truly is. It can nevertheless alter who people are to one another.

## 10. Worked application: commitment and retrospective standards

Suppose two partners explicitly organize a relationship around equality and reject a protector-dependent arrangement. A crisis later reveals that one partner possessed an unarticulated embodied expectation that the other would accept disproportionate danger.

The newly discovered expectation is real. It may make continued relationship impossible. But it cannot retroactively become a term the other party knowingly breached.

Later knowledge can change what happens next. It cannot change what another person could reasonably have known or owed beforehand.

This exposes several distinctions:

- practical power to leave;
- protection against coercion;
- moral justification for leaving;
- discovery of a previously illegible need;
- breach of a previously shared obligation;
- fair allocation of the cost of discovery.

It also exposes moral hazard. A person may receive the ordinary benefits of one norm while retrospectively invoking an incompatible norm when advantageous. Sincere self-ignorance can explain this without making the resulting cost allocation fair.

Accountability may therefore require the formulation:

> You acted under an understanding I helped establish. I have discovered that I cannot live with its consequences.

Law may permit dissolution without adjudicating this history. That is a deliberate legal telos: manageable termination rather than complete moral interpretation. But institutional non-adjudication can alter incentives. If faithful investment creates vulnerability while strategic repudiation creates advantage, the surrounding practice may move toward a different attractor while retaining the same name.

This case generalizes. Whenever institutions decline to interpret contested obligations, we must ask whether they merely avoid enforcement, externalize costs, or actively reward repudiation.

## 11. Recognition is not transformation

A system can accurately represent its failure while continuing to produce it.

\[
\boxed{\text{recognition}\neq\text{transformation}}
\]

Knowing, wanting, inhibiting, acting, and retaining change are distinct capacities. Addiction supplies the cleanest example: a person may understand the pattern, sincerely desire change, interrupt it for months, and relapse when stress or context reactivates an older pathway.

Metacognition adds awareness of cognition. Reflexivity recognizes participation in the observed process. Neither is sufficient unless the representation gains causal access to attention, inhibition, action selection, environment, reinforcement, memory, and future interpretation.

Transformation requires conditions under which feedback can first exist and reach the system. Consequences may be hidden, testimony suppressed, measures selected to conceal failure, or language denied in which an anomaly could be expressed. The zeroth question is therefore:

> Can action generate an accessible counter-signal, and can that signal reach the system without being destroyed or systematically filtered?

Transformation then requires a correction cycle:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Feedback availability}
\rightarrow
\text{Reception}
\rightarrow
\text{Verification}
\rightarrow
\text{Integration}
\rightarrow
\text{Enactment}
\rightarrow
\text{Retention}
}
\]

### Feedback availability

Can consequences become detectable and communicable?

- Deficiency: concealed costs, silenced testimony, corrupted measures, inaccessible records, punishment for reporting.
- Excess: indiscriminate signalling, unranked metrics, and feedback systems so noisy that consequence cannot be distinguished from fluctuation.

### Reception

Can a corrective signal enter the system?

- Deficiency: denial, inattention, avoidance.
- Excess: flooding, hypervigilance, inability to filter noise.

### Verification

Can the signal be evaluated?

- Deficiency: gullibility, authority capture, motivated acceptance.
- Excess: compulsive doubt, infinite demands for proof, paralysis.

### Integration

Can the correction alter the existing orientation without destroying all continuity?

- Deficiency: fragmentation, compartmentalization, identity rupture.
- Excess: forced coherence, confabulation, total explanation, apophenia.

### Enactment

Can the revised orientation modify action?

- Deficiency: akrasia, paralysis, helplessness, insight without change.
- Excess: impulsivity and action before evidence stabilizes.

### Retention

Can the transformation survive time, stress, and context?

- Deficiency: forgetting, relapse, state-dependent learning, decay.
- Excess: fixation, rigidity, retention after conditions change.

Retention changes future reception. Renewed monitoring tests whether the retained correction still fits altered conditions. The cycle is recursive rather than linear.

Articulation operates across the cycle rather than occupying one compulsory stage. Some anomalies are felt before they can be named; some corrections are tested in action before they become explicit; some institutions prevent correction precisely by monopolizing the language in which failure could become legible.

## 12. Interruptibility and recursive agency

A process is interruptible when it can:

1. detect a counter-signal;
2. suspend its trajectory;
3. preserve time for evaluation;
4. access an alternative;
5. switch without disintegrating.

The available interruption window can be expressed as:

\[
W=t_{commitment}-(t_{detection}+t_{inhibition})
\]

If \(W\leq0\), recognition arrives only after action has become committed.

Maximum interruptibility is not health. A system that reopens every conclusion cannot sustain coordinated action. The target is **selective interruptibility**:

> Stable without being captive; corrigible without being permanently undecided.

Interruptibility is not another stage of correction. It is a cross-cutting control property: the capacity to pause, reopen, or redirect reception, verification, integration, enactment, or retention when counter-signals reach sufficient importance.

This leads to recursive agency.

Agency is not only choosing among present possibilities. It is participating in the construction of the future self that will perceive, evaluate, and generate possibilities.

\[
S_{t+1}=U(S_t,\text{experience},\text{relationship},\text{reflection})
\]

At a deeper level:

\[
U_{t+1}=V(U_t,\text{meta-reflection},\text{consequence})
\]

The person does not create themselves from nothing. Existing capacities, which were not chosen, are used to alter some conditions under which future choices will occur.

Three relative orders of intervention follow:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{First order:}&\quad\text{change an output}\\
\text{Second order:}&\quad\text{change the operator producing outputs}\\
\text{Third order:}&\quad\text{change the possibility landscape}
\end{aligned}
\]

These are not final ontological layers. They are indexed to the selected baseline. Every possibility landscape can itself become an object of wider inquiry.

Orientation is also coupled across scales:

\[
O^{task}
\leftrightarrow
O^{role}
\leftrightarrow
O^{identity}
\leftrightarrow
O^{institution}
\leftrightarrow
O^{culture}
\leftrightarrow
O^{cosmos}
\]

These are not literal sets contained inside one another. They are partially overlapping organizations with bidirectional constraint. An anomaly may remain local or propagate across levels. A correction can resolve one level while damaging another. Addiction can secure short-term affective order while degrading life orientation. Bureaucratic compliance can preserve institutional order while betraying professional telos. Conversion can reorganize several levels simultaneously.

Cross-scale viability therefore means that an orientation does not preserve coherence at one level merely by exporting unacknowledged disorder to other persons, institutions, future selves, or generations.

## 13. Culture as normative exoskeleton

No individual independently discovers how to speak, promise, exchange, restrain aggression, raise children, distribute authority, mourn, reconcile, or die. Culture supplies a normative exoskeleton within which agency develops.

But culture is not merely a field of impersonal norms. Concrete others address, recognize, trust, accuse, forgive, betray, teach, and depend upon us. A rule may specify what partners generally owe; a person can still say, “You did this to me.” Accountability, belonging, and identity therefore require a relational field that cannot be reduced to norm compliance.

Recognition matters in at least three ways:

- others partly confer and contest identity;
- testimony can disclose consequences unavailable from the actor's perspective;
- repair requires renewed relationship, not merely corrected classification.

Norms coordinate:

- attention;
- expectation;
- trust;
- emotional expression;
- role differentiation;
- time horizon;
- sexuality and kinship;
- authority;
- conflict and repair;
- relations between living, dead, and unborn.

Their load-bearing function is often invisible because successful norms prevent the failures that would reveal their purpose. A norm isolated as a proposition may appear arbitrary while remaining essential within a network of expectations.

The appropriate presumption is:

> A surviving norm has earned investigation, not obedience.

Its persistence is evidence that it performed some function under some conditions. Inquiry must determine:

- what function;
- for whom;
- at whose expense;
- under which ecology;
- whether that ecology persists;
- whether the function can be inherited without the cost.

This is Chesterton's fence expressed as systems analysis.

Culture must preserve two capacities that remain in tension:

- conservation of accumulated adaptation;
- exposure to corrective anomaly.

Pure preservation fossilizes. Pure disruption destroys knowledge faster than it can recognize it.

## 14. Authority, education, and formation

Culture is the inherited field of norms, practices, symbols, and capacities. **Authority is the routing and weighting function within that field:** it determines whose signals, interpretations, and directives count. It shapes every stage of correction by determining:

- which signals become receivable;
- which sources require verification;
- whose interpretation supplies coherence;
- which actions are permitted;
- which lessons are institutionally retained.

Authority is not the opposite of freedom. No person can verify everything independently. Trustworthy authority expands practical agency by transmitting capacities, preserving knowledge, and coordinating action.

Authority becomes pathological when it exempts itself from the processes by which error could be detected:

\[
\text{authority claim}
\rightarrow
\text{verification bypass}
\rightarrow
\text{forced integration}
\]

Education and indoctrination use the same materials: trust, imitation, repetition, attachment, narrative, reward, and social belonging.

Their provisional distinction is:

> Education forms a person while cultivating a future capacity to examine that formation. Indoctrination forms a person while capturing or disabling the machinery of examination.

This distinction is not absolute. Unlimited questioning can become paralysis or strategic disruption. Communities require boundaries; threats may be real; no curriculum is neutral; deep exit is never costless.

The correct unit is therefore nested:

- person;
- family;
- community;
- institution;
- state.

Individual and collective self-authorship can genuinely conflict. Boundaries must be judged by their scope, proportionality, evidence, consistency, reviewability, relation to conduct rather than private belief, and capacity for correction.

## 15. Myth, archetype, and the collective unconscious

Myth is compressed dynamical knowledge. It carries transformations that may have been enacted long before they could be explicitly theorized.

A candidate invariant grammar is:

\[
\text{bounded order}
\rightarrow
\text{excluded remainder}
\rightarrow
\text{anomaly}
\rightarrow
\text{failed defence}
\rightarrow
\text{descent or sacrifice}
\rightarrow
\text{discovery}
\rightarrow
\text{return}
\rightarrow
\text{renewed order}
\]

Mythic figures personify positions within this transformation:

- old king: established order losing adaptive capacity;
- tyrant: order suppressing corrective reality;
- dragon: dangerous, unintegrated possibility;
- hero: voluntary attention entering excluded reality;
- treasure: otherwise inaccessible adaptive information;
- miraculous child: new order not derivable from the exhausted terms of the old;
- death and resurrection: identity discontinuity within deeper continuity;
- divine word: articulation converting undifferentiated possibility into inhabitable distinction.

Archetypes need not initially be treated as inherited pictures. The collective unconscious may be an attractor landscape generated by:

- shared embodiment;
- mammalian affect;
- dependency and development;
- sexual reproduction and kinship;
- status, coalition, and conflict;
- mortality;
- attentional architecture;
- recurrent coordination problems;
- the formal structure of agency.

Archetypes would then be stable solutions in the space of possible symbolic representations. The stronger Jungian hypothesis—that some organizing dispositions are innate—becomes empirically investigable rather than assumed or dismissed.

## 16. Logos as the architecture of correction

The correction cycle is descriptively neutral: propaganda, trauma, coercion, advertising, and addiction can all transform an orientation. The Logos names a normatively ordered form of transformation. It is the meta-process by which finite orientations remain answerable to reality, other persons, and excluded consequence without losing all continuity.

It requires:

1. reception of what the current order excludes;
2. verification without either credulity or infinite suspicion;
3. articulation of the anomaly;
4. discrimination between local and structural failure;
5. proportionate sacrifice of inadequate order;
6. integration into identity and action;
7. social negotiation and institutional embodiment;
8. retention across time and stress;
9. preserved capacity for future reopening.

Logos is not mere speech, calculation, scepticism, or rebellion. It is **continuity through truthful transformation**. Correction describes how change occurs; Logos names the quality of correction when reception is honest, verification is proportionate, articulation preserves the anomaly, sacrifice is not displaced onto the powerless, action embodies the revision, and the result remains reopenable.

The hero is the person or subsystem capable of entering what the established order cannot assimilate and returning with a transformation that renews the whole.

The adversary is not merely someone who states false propositions. It is the process that protects local coherence by disabling correction: hiding evidence, capturing attention, monopolizing hope, degrading language, punishing messengers, externalizing costs, and making the map immune to the territory.

## 17. Pathologies of orientation: capture and idolatry

Many apparently different pathologies share one formal structure: a partial process captures authority over the whole and suppresses the feedback by which its limits would become visible.

### Addiction

A short-horizon policy overrides wider commitments, alters salience, recruits identity, and survives explicit recognition through cue-dependent pathways and reinforcement.

### Cultic control

An institution coordinates control over behaviour, information, interpretation, identity, relationship, dependency, and exit. It does not merely supply beliefs; it captures the machinery by which beliefs could be corrected.

### Advertising

Deep advertising does not merely satisfy existing desire. It authors a possibility landscape:

\[
\text{identity ideal}
\rightarrow
\text{felt deficiency}
\rightarrow
\text{product as repair}
\]

### Ideological possession

A doctrine fuses with identity and belonging, converting anomaly into threat and criticism into evidence of enemy alignment.

### Idolatry

A finite object, person, institution, or representation acquires authority over the standards by which it could be judged. The needle declares itself north.

These systems often capture affective resources:

- **Hope:** the desired future becomes available only through the authority.
- **Love:** attachment becomes conditional upon epistemic surrender.
- **Momentum:** past sacrifice increases the cost of correction.

\[
\text{hope}
\rightarrow
\text{commitment}
\rightarrow
\text{sacrifice}
\rightarrow
\text{identity}
\rightarrow
\text{exit cost}
\rightarrow
\text{greater commitment}
\]

## 18. Moral truth as invariance across transformations

The method introduced earlier—seeking invariants across transformations—can now be applied to moral judgement. Moral truth need not require an impossible perspective outside every frame. It may concern what survives legitimate transformations of perspective.

A moral order can be tested by transformations such as:

- reversing actor and recipient;
- equalizing access to relevant information;
- extending the time horizon;
- applying rules to authorities as well as subjects;
- including externalized costs;
- hearing affected outsiders;
- allowing suppressed information to become visible;
- asking whether dissent can occur without annihilation;
- asking whether the order preserves the conditions of its own moral learning.

The word “legitimate” cannot be allowed to decide the result in advance. Candidate transformations should be stated publicly, applied reciprocally, made as reversible as the subject permits, and compared for what each reveals or conceals. A transformation may itself distort the phenomenon: exchanging social positions in imagination, for example, does not automatically reproduce differences in embodiment, history, dependency, or risk. Transformation tests constrain moral judgement; they do not mechanically generate it.

Candidate invariants include:

- reciprocity;
- corrigibility;
- non-parasitism;
- protection of agency;
- truthful cost accounting;
- proportionality;
- capacity for reconciliation;
- intergenerational generativity;
- integration rather than export of disorder.

A fanatic can be perfectly consistent within a closed frame. The question is whether coherence survives transformation of frame or depends on censorship, asymmetric standing, hidden cost, and irreversible exclusion.

Moral objectivity may therefore resemble objectivity elsewhere: not absence of perspective, but discovery of invariants across disciplined transformations of perspective.

## 19. Legibility, institutions, and official reality

Two forms of legibility must be distinguished here. **Epistemic legibility** concerns whether something can be represented at all. **Institutional legibility** concerns whether a representation becomes authoritative and actionable within a system. Power enters decisively at the second level.

Legibility is therefore never only descriptive. Whoever makes experience institutionally legible can influence:

- its category;
- its authority;
- its clinical status;
- its educational transmission;
- its legal consequences;
- its place in official memory.

Institutions necessarily compress reality. Law cannot adjudicate every moral truth. Diagnosis cannot represent the whole person. Administrative categories cannot contain every lived difference. The question is which losses the institution acknowledges, which it hides, and which incentives its simplification creates.

Power operates at several relative orders:

- first order: determine an action or outcome;
- second order: determine the rule selecting outcomes;
- third order: determine the possibility landscape and the standards by which rules are judged.

Power can be defined more generally as:

> The differential capacity to structure another agent's field of possible orientation and action.

This capacity can be material, relational, informational, institutional, or coercive. Food, shelter, money, bodily safety, mobility, technology, time, and access to communication are not merely background conditions. They determine which practical repertoires are real. Two people may interpret a situation identically while possessing radically different possibility landscapes.

Third-order victory acquires control over courts, archives, borders, categories, curricula, citizenship, and official memory. Conquest becomes settlement; settlement becomes institution; institution becomes normality; normality becomes law.

The victor cannot make falsehood true. But the victor may determine which truth receives enforcement, monuments, curricula, borders, and durable institutional memory.

Two propositions can coexist:

\[
\text{an act was genuinely wrong}
\]

and

\[
\text{its prosecution, unlike another wrong, was selected by power}
\]

Recognizing the second must not dissolve the first.

## 20. Collective application: totalitarianism as a stable basin

Totalitarianism is not simply maximal state power or widespread false belief. It is a self-stabilizing correction failure:

\[
\text{threat}
\rightarrow
\text{narrowed attention}
\rightarrow
\text{identity fusion}
\rightarrow
\text{conformity}
\rightarrow
\text{suppressed anomaly}
\rightarrow
\text{model degradation}
\rightarrow
\text{material failure}
\rightarrow
\text{greater threat}
\]

Characteristic indicators include:

- moralization of epistemic disagreement;
- expanding enemy categories;
- ritual demonstrations of commitment;
- fusion of factual judgement with loyalty;
- disappearance of protected local experimentation;
- widening divergence between private and public speech;
- punishment of messengers;
- policy failure interpreted as sabotage;
- transfer of decision authority to those least exposed to correction.

The basin exhibits hysteresis. Removal of the initiating threat does not automatically restore openness because identity, institutions, incentives, and fear have been reorganized around the closed system.

Escape therefore requires more than contrary facts. It requires protected dissent, non-doctrinal identities, trusted procedures for admitting error, local experiments, norms of reconciliation, and a future into which participants can move without total humiliation.

The danger is reflexive: interpreting all influence as war can justify the very capture one fears.

## 21. Clinical application: psychology and psychiatry have misidentified their object

Modern psychology and psychiatry have achieved real technical successes within an impoverished ontology of the person.

They commonly isolate:

- traits;
- symptom clusters;
- behavioural outputs;
- cognitive modules;
- neural correlates;
- individual treatment responses.

But the relevant object may be the organized person-world relation distributed across brain, body, attention, relationship, norm, institution, narrative, and time.

A symptom may simultaneously be:

- biological dysfunction;
- learned adaptation;
- attentional capture;
- relational signal;
- identity defence;
- response to contradictory norms;
- evidence that the available world contains no viable policy.

The relevant distinctions must remain open:

\[
\text{variation}
\neq
\text{trait}
\neq
\text{state}
\neq
\text{symptom}
\neq
\text{syndrome}
\neq
\text{disorder}
\]

Two opposite errors follow:

- **medicalization:** treating difference, dissent, or an intelligible response to adversity as individual disease;
- **romanticization:** treating severe addiction, psychosis, mania, or disabling distress entirely as social construction or valuable difference.

An orientation-based clinical formulation would ask:

> What world does this person inhabit? How is it disclosed by attention? Which goods organize it? What identity holds it coherent? Which norms and authorities maintain it? Where does correction fail? What transformation could restore agency without destroying continuity?

Treatment would not abandon symptoms or biology. It would locate them inside the person-in-a-field-over-time.

## 22. Metaphysical interpretation: the Christian Logos hypothesis

Psychology can describe the Logos pattern without explaining why reality should reward it.

Christian metaphysics makes a stronger proposal:

> Logos is not merely an adaptive human strategy. Reality is ultimately such that truthful, self-giving participation reveals and renews being.

Within that interpretation:

- creation through the Word presents intelligible differentiation as fundamental;
- the image of God presents each person as a potential locus of creative moral renewal;
- the Fall presents consciousness divided through grasping appropriation;
- incarnation presents truth as embodied rather than merely asserted;
- crucifixion presents the highest order refusing to preserve itself through domination and falsehood;
- resurrection presents a renewed order unavailable to defensive preservation;
- the Spirit presents renewal as living and distributed rather than monopolized by established form.

Psychological correspondence does not deductively prove Christian metaphysics. But neither is it irrelevant. If a theological grammar repeatedly integrates structures otherwise fragmented across psychology, ethics, history, phenomenology, and ritual, this contributes abductive evidence.

Psychology maps the recurring landscape. Theology asks why the landscape has that shape.

## 23. The integrated process

The core architecture can now be stated without conflating orientation, context, transformation, and evaluation.

### State, context, and update

\[
O_t=(D_t,G_t,L_t,\Pi_t)

\[
C_t=(W_t,B_t,R_t,N_t,H_t)
\]

\[
O_{t+1}=U(O_t,E_t,X_t;C_t)
\]

Orientation contains disclosure, guidance, location, and practical repertoire. Context contains world, embodiment, relationships, norms, and history. The update process includes encounter, action, consequence, and correction. Corrigibility and moral warrant evaluate the quality of this process; they are not additional descriptive components.

The lived architecture can be compressed into one recursive arc:

\[
\begin{aligned}
&\text{encounter or presence}\\
\rightarrow{}&\text{attention, attunement, and affective salience}\\
\rightarrow{}&\text{orientation within telos and ranked goods}\\
\rightarrow{}&\text{identity, role, and recognition}\\
\rightarrow{}&\text{judgement}\\
\rightarrow{}&\text{action and sacrifice}\\
\rightarrow{}&\text{consequence}\\
\rightarrow{}&\text{description and recognition}\\
\rightarrow{}&\text{accountability}\\
\rightarrow{}&\text{feedback, reception, verification, integration, enactment, retention}\\
\rightarrow{}&\text{transformed identity and agency}\\
\rightarrow{}&\text{norm, institution, and inherited culture}\\
\rightarrow{}&\text{the attentional world of future agents}\\
\rightarrow{}&\text{new encounter}
\end{aligned}
\]

This contains two coupled loops.

### The personal-transformative loop

\[
\text{encounter}
\rightarrow
\text{orientation}
\rightarrow
\text{action}
\rightarrow
\text{correction}
\rightarrow
\text{identity transformation}
\]

### The cultural-institutional loop

\[
\text{repeated action}
\rightarrow
\text{norm}
\rightarrow
\text{institution}
\rightarrow
\text{legibility and authority}
\rightarrow
\text{formation of future agents}
\]

Personal change becomes cultural form. Cultural form becomes the perceptual and practical environment of later persons. Power enters because institutions determine which encounters count, which descriptions become official, which corrections are permitted, and which identities remain possible.

The two loops are coupled but not identical. The personal loop can change without institutional sedimentation; institutional forms can persist after the experiences that originally justified them have disappeared. This lag is one source of both cultural memory and cultural pathology.

## 24. Reflexive audit: the architecture applied to itself

A theory of corrigible orientation must submit its own orientation to the processes it recommends. This is not satisfied by adding a disclaimer that the theory is provisional. The representation must identify its field, interests, exclusions, correction bottlenecks, and conditions of abandonment.

### What does the document disclose?

The document directs broad, relational attention toward connections commonly separated by disciplinary boundaries. It makes visible:

- the dependence of perception on attention and telos;
- the relation between tacit knowledge and explicit representation;
- the propagation of anomaly into identity;
- the difference between recognition and retained transformation;
- the sedimentation of personal action into norms and institutions;
- the role of power in controlling feedback and official description;
- structural similarities among addiction, ideological capture, institutional rigidity, and idolatry.

This integrative attention is its principal strength. It allows phenomena at different scales to become mutually illuminating.

### What does its mode of attention suppress?

The same integrative tendency creates systematic blind spots.

First, the document is biased toward pattern and continuity. It may perceive an invariant where there is only analogy, translate genuine difference into another instance of its cycle, or treat contradiction as an invitation to add another level rather than abandon a claim.

Second, its preferred response to criticism is “yes, and.” This is generative, but a framework capable of absorbing every objection by expanding itself is not corrigible. Some anomalies must be allowed to subtract, partition, or falsify.

Third, the document remains predominantly semantic and structural. Embodiment, material scarcity, technology, violence, illness, and ecological constraint are named, but not yet given explanatory detail proportional to their causal force.

Fourth, the document inherits a particular intellectual formation: English-language psychology and philosophy, cybernetic language, Jungian and Petersonian mythology, McGilchrist's attentional distinctions, and Christian theological interpretation. Calling these sources universal would repeat the very passage from situated representation to official reality that the document warns against.

Fifth, “the Other” still risks becoming a variable inside the architecture. A concrete person can refuse the theory's description, contest the goods it assumes, or remain partly unintelligible. Their resistance is not merely unintegrated data awaiting absorption.

### Meaning and identity of the document

The manuscript has high internal meaning:

- **Coherence:** many conversations now occupy one vocabulary.
- **Purpose:** it aims to recover the person-in-a-field-over-time as an object of inquiry.
- **Mattering:** it promises relevance across psychology, culture, ethics, politics, and theology.

These strengths create identity risk. The document can begin to understand itself as the missing meta-framework within which rival accounts find their proper place. Once it adopts that identity, evidence against it can be redescribed as local truth requiring integration rather than genuine refutation.

Its most dangerous possible self-description is:

> Other theories describe fragments; this architecture explains how the fragments fit.

That claim may sometimes be fruitful. Treated as an entitlement, it makes the framework hierarchically immune.

### Correction-cycle audit

#### Feedback availability

Current feedback is drawn primarily from conversation, conceptual tension, and internal comparison. The theory has not yet been exposed to systematic cross-cultural data, clinical prediction, adversarial expert review, or failed intervention. Its feedback environment is therefore rich in ideas but poor in recalcitrant consequence.

#### Reception

The architecture is highly receptive to conceptual additions. Its greater risk is excessive reception: incorporating every relevant concept until boundedness disappears.

#### Verification

Verification is presently the weakest stage. Much of the document consists of plausible synthesis. Definitions, hypotheses, normative claims, and metaphysical interpretations were not initially marked clearly enough, allowing coherence at one level to lend authority to another.

#### Integration

Integration is the strongest stage and therefore the likeliest site of excess. The document can force coherence by translating distinct phenomena into orientation language. Similarity of vocabulary may then be mistaken for identity of mechanism.

#### Enactment

The theory has produced a manuscript and a research programme, but not yet a validated measure, comparative model, clinical formulation protocol, institutional analysis, or intervention. Its practical repertoire remains largely redescriptive.

#### Retention

The written architecture preserves revisions, but empirical retention is unknown. It has not shown that people or institutions using it make better predictions, undergo more durable correction, or avoid the capture it describes.

### Power and institutional legibility

The vocabulary makes some phenomena newly legible, but every gain in legibility creates power. Terms such as “incorrigible,” “captured,” “identity-defensive,” or “low reality-contact” could be used to disqualify disagreement while presenting the judgement as clinical or structural rather than political.

The framework must therefore not grant its interpreter unilateral authority to diagnose another person's orientation. Application should preserve:

- the subject's capacity to contest the description;
- competing formulations in their strongest available form;
- separation of descriptive, clinical, moral, and political judgements;
- disclosure of who bears the costs of an intervention;
- symmetry where symmetry is possible;
- acknowledgement where asymmetry is materially real.

The architecture should increase answerability, not become a new official language through which one party monopolizes the meaning of another's resistance.

### Theology and circular confirmation

The Christian Logos hypothesis is especially vulnerable to confirmation by construction. If Logos is defined using qualities associated with the Christian narrative and the Christian narrative is then praised for exemplifying Logos, the result is circular.

The hypothesis becomes stronger only if:

- Logos is specified independently enough to permit non-Christian instantiations;
- rival metaphysical interpretations are compared at equal resolution;
- Christian history is permitted to supply counterevidence as well as illustration;
- psychological usefulness is not treated as ontological proof;
- the theology generates insight or prediction not inserted through the original definition.

### Conditions of failure

The architecture should be weakened, partitioned, or abandoned if research shows that:

- attentional mode does not reliably alter perceived affordances or significance beyond ordinary task selection;
- identity centrality adds no explanatory power to responses to anomaly;
- the proposed correction functions do not distinguish different failure trajectories or improve prediction;
- mythic transformation patterns disappear after adequate controls for diffusion, genre, and coding flexibility;
- orientation measures add no value beyond existing constructs such as goals, schemas, identity, personality, social support, and predictive processing;
- cross-scale language produces analogy but no transferable predictions;
- interventions derived from the framework perform no better than simpler formulations;
- the framework's classifications prove less contestable, less reliable, or more coercive than those they replace.

Failure of one claim need not destroy the whole. But the architecture must specify in advance which component fails rather than redescribing every result as another manifestation of complexity.

### Revisions produced by the audit

Applying the document to itself requires the following commitments:

1. Keep orientation state, constitutive context, update process, evaluation, application, and metaphysical interpretation distinct.
2. Mark the epistemic status of major claims.
3. Maintain an anomaly ledger containing evidence that does not integrate cleanly.
4. Require subtraction: every major revision must ask what should be removed, not only what can be added.
5. Compare the architecture against simpler and rival explanations.
6. Predefine component-level failure conditions.
7. Preserve the standing of affected persons to contest how the framework describes them.
8. Treat embodiment and material power as causal constraints rather than decorative acknowledgements.
9. Prevent Christian interpretation from borrowing certainty from psychological recurrence.
10. Apply capture analysis first to the framework, its authors, and its institutional uses.

### Provisional judgement

The architecture currently succeeds as a generative conceptual map. It has not yet earned the status of an explanatory theory. Its strongest warranted claim is that orientation provides a promising common unit for relating attention, value, identity, action, correction, and normative context. Its weakest tendency is total integration: the very capacity that gives it scope could make it an elegant system for evading refutation.

Its next developmental task is therefore not further expansion. It is disciplined exposure to exclusion, comparison, measurement, and possible loss.

## 25. A research programme

The architecture becomes serious only where it risks failure.

### Attention and disclosed world

Manipulate broad and narrow attention and measure changes in perceived affordances, context sensitivity, moral interpretation, memory, ambiguity tolerance, and exploratory action.

### Identity-level anomaly

Compare responses to peripheral, belief-central, identity-central, and norm-central contradiction. Test whether response severity depends on dependency depth rather than mere factual magnitude.

### Correction-cycle bottlenecks

Measure reception, verification, integration, enactment, and retention separately. Determine whether clinical relapse and institutional rigidity can be predicted by stage-specific failure.

### Norm networks

Study norm change as intervention in a dependency network. Track delayed effects on trust, coordination, exploitation, role clarity, and replacement norms.

### Mythic invariants

Construct cross-cultural narrative corpora encoded as transformations rather than motifs alone. Control for diffusion and ecology. Compare control-theoretic, developmental, coalitional, and historical explanations.

### Collective-unconscious hypothesis

Examine spontaneous childhood symbolism, dreams, convergent invention, narrative memorability, developmental timing, and cross-cultural recurrence to distinguish learned content from constrained symbolic attractors.

### Accountability and institutional legibility

Compare moral histories with the simplified categories used by law, diagnosis, and administration. Identify where simplification avoids coercion, externalizes cost, or creates perverse reward.

### Totalitarian basin

Use historical and contemporary data to test predicted feedback among threat, attention, identity fusion, enforcement, information degradation, policy failure, and hysteresis.

### Clinical orientation

Develop measures of attentional flexibility, goal hierarchy, identity continuity, normative conflict, social role viability, anomaly integration, and corrigibility. Test whether they add predictive value beyond diagnosis and symptom severity.

### Comparative adequacy and anomaly ledger

For each domain, compare the orientation architecture with simpler models and established alternatives. Record findings that resist translation into orientation language, including null results, scale discontinuities, culture-specific structures, and cases where the framework adds vocabulary without improving prediction. Revisions must state both what is added and what is removed.

### Reflexive application

Apply the theory to its authors, adherents, measures, and institutional uses. Track whether criticism changes component claims, merely expands the architecture, or is redescribed as evidence of the critic's capture. A theory of corrigibility that cannot lose claims has already failed.

## Conclusion

Human knowledge does not consist only in representing what is true. It includes the forms by which persons and communities:

- encounter significance;
- learn what deserves attention;
- become attuned to differences;
- orient themselves toward ends;
- rank goods;
- act and sacrifice;
- recognize consequences;
- remain answerable across time;
- transform insight into retained change;
- transmit norms and capacities;
- govern boundaries;
- and preserve the possibility of correcting what they have built.

Orientation is the temporally extended relation through which an embodied and socially formed agent is disclosed a field of significance, located within it, guided among its possibilities, enabled to act, and capable—or incapable—of being transformed by consequence.

The central virtue is neither openness nor stability considered alone. It is the achieved relation between them.

The central pathology is capture: a partial representation, impulse, authority, institution, or identity acquires control over the conditions by which it could be corrected.

The manuscript is exposed to the same pathology. Its breadth is warranted only where it produces discriminating understanding, survives comparison, and permits evidence to remove claims. Otherwise “orientation” becomes the kind of self-authorizing official language the architecture was designed to diagnose.

The central moral and political question is:

> Can this person, relationship, tradition, institution, or civilization act decisively, retain what works, receive what it excludes, and reopen the conditions of its own judgement without disintegrating or making itself sovereign over reality?

The name for that capacity is **corrigible orientation**. It is a quality and achievement of orientation, not orientation's definition.

The mythic name for its enactment is **the hero**.

The theological name for its ultimate ground is **Logos**.
