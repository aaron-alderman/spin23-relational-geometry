# Kenotic Governance: Tri-Omni Theism and Shared Responsibility in a Lawful World

**Date:** 23 October 2026

**Status:** philosophical and theological synthesis; constructive argument, not a physical derivation or historical proof

## Abstract

This article develops a kenotic account of a tri-omni God: a God who is maximally powerful, fully knowing, and perfectly good. The central proposal is that divine capability does not entail either moral permission or a standing obligation to exercise every available power. Perfect goodness governs omnipotence through non-dominating love. God creates a stable but open world in which natural law, creaturely agency, moral formation, reciprocal relationship, and reasonable epistemic disagreement are real; ordinarily permits actions to have consequences; acts without monopolizing the causal or evidential field; and entrusts creatures with genuine responsibility for protecting one another. This account interprets divine wrath partly through the natural, relational, and institutional consequences of destructive action, while refusing to identify suffering with guilt. It also retains an irreducible category of tragedy: suffering not presently shown to be necessary, proportionate, or justified. Incarnation is the bridge by which God participates in this tragedy, the cross discloses power as self-giving rather than domination, and resurrection grounds restoration without retroactively declaring suffering good. To avoid becoming self-sealing, the model adopts explicit criteria for intervention, hiddenness, adverse evidence, and burden of proof. It offers a coherent and defeasible theology of divine action, not a proof that every actual non-intervention or degree of hiddenness was optimal.

## The question behind the title God

The word *god* has been used for beings of widely varying scope: local deities, members of pantheons, finite supernatural agents, political rulers, and the ultimate ground of reality. That semantic plurality does not answer the normative question of what kind of being would be worthy of unconditional worship and the fullest title *God*. The present argument adopts a perfect-being criterion: only a being unsurpassable in knowledge, power, and goodness is worthy of that title in the ultimate sense.

The familiar tri-omni conception can be stated more carefully than the slogan that God knows everything, can do anything, and is wholly good:

- **Omniscience:** God knows every truth that is logically knowable, including the consequences and opportunity costs of possible actions.
- **Omnipotence:** God can actualize every intrinsically possible state consistent with God's nature and commitments.
- **Omnibenevolence:** God acts from perfect love and never treats a person merely as an instrument.

The apparent contradiction between such a God and a world containing hiddenness, predation, natural suffering, and moral horror arises largely at the point where omnipotence is equated with exhaustive control. Kenotic theology denies that equation:

\[
\text{capability}
\not\Rightarrow
\text{moral permission}
\not\Rightarrow
\text{obligation to exercise}.
\]

Power describes what God can do. Goodness governs how that power may be used. A God who can determine every outcome need not be morally entitled to replace every created choice with the divine preference. Even legitimate authority does not imply that every permissible exercise of control would be loving.

## Kenosis as principled non-domination

Kenosis is often described as divine self-emptying. For the present argument it requires a more precise definition:

> **Kenotic restraint is God's principled refusal to exercise available power in ways that would unnecessarily replace, overwhelm, or trivialize the agency of created beings.**

Kenosis is therefore not divine incapacity. Nor is it absence, passivity, or an absolute prohibition against intervention. It is a discipline internal to perfect power: the refusal to secure good outcomes by abolishing the otherness of the beings for whom those outcomes are intended.

Three commitments give this discipline its moral form:

- **Unconditional love:** the creature's worth and God's orientation toward its good are not withdrawn when it refuses God.
- **Boundaries:** love does not require unlimited access, immunity from consequences, or permission to consume another person's freedom.
- **Power serving:** power exists to protect, enable, restore, and reconcile rather than to absorb every other agency into itself.

The deepest contrast is not between power and weakness but between dominating and serving power. Kenotic omnipotence is power governed by love strongly enough to make room for what is not God.

## Creation as a narrow path between chaos and closure

A world capable of sustaining finite agents cannot be entirely chaotic. Identity, memory, prediction, responsibility, inquiry, and cumulative knowledge require stable regularities. But a world whose complete history is exhaustively fixed in a way that leaves no effective openness appears equally hostile to genuine novelty, participation, and consequential agency.

Creation therefore occupies a viable region between two failures:

\[
\text{sterile closure}
\;\longleftarrow\;
\boxed{\text{lawful, intelligible, generative freedom}}
\;\longrightarrow\;
\text{formless chaos}.
\]

This proposal is more than the ordinary observation that observers can arise only in observer-compatible conditions. It is a **participatory selection principle**: creation must sustain beings capable not merely of existing, but of understanding, acting, bearing consequences, entering relationships, and contributing novel history.

Let \(\mathcal M\) be a space of metaphysically possible worlds. The relevant viability region can be represented schematically as

\[
\mathcal V
=
\mathcal I
\cap\mathcal S
\cap\mathcal A
\cap\mathcal G
\cap\mathcal R,
\]

where \(\mathcal I\) denotes intelligibility, \(\mathcal S\) stability, \(\mathcal A\) effective agency, \(\mathcal G\) generativity, and \(\mathcal R\) relational accessibility without domination. The thesis is not that these conditions uniquely determine one universe. It is that they constrain the class of worlds suitable for kenotic creation.

Natural law then has a dual significance. It constrains creatures by making actions consequential, but it also expresses a divine commitment not to make every event transparently responsive to immediate divine preference. An entirely magical world would not necessarily compel love; unmistakable knowledge does not logically determine affection. Its deeper defect would be the destruction of dependable creaturely causation. If any event could change arbitrarily, finite beings could not reliably learn how their actions matter.

The language of creation as a divine speaking act captures this structure. God does not need to dictate every sentence of history. God establishes a grammar within which creatures can make consequential statements of their own:

\[
\text{space of possibilities}
\xrightarrow{\text{creative address}}
\text{a lawful world with open history}.
\]

Talk of possibility being "collapsed" should remain metaphysical unless a physical account is supplied. It does not by itself imply quantum wave-function collapse or any other specific mechanism.

## Lawful openness and room for divine action

Kenotic creation must not quietly become deism. If the lawful world leaves room for creaturely action but none for divine action, relationship is reduced to divine withdrawal. The alternative is not a competition between natural law and supernatural interruption, but a world described by

\[
\boxed{\text{stable constraints}
+\text{underdetermined realization}
+\text{non-dominating participation}}.
\]

The laws specify an admissible structure without necessarily specifying every realization. Within that openness, creatures contribute genuine local causes, chance may contribute outcomes not fixed by antecedent conditions, and God may call, guide, coordinate, disclose, heal, or judge without treating creation as a puppet.

A schematic transition rule is

\[
S_{t+1}
=
F\!\left(S_t,
A_{\mathrm{creature}},
A_{\mathrm{chance}},
A_{\mathrm{divine}};
\mathcal L\right),
\]

where \(\mathcal L\) is the lawful admissibility structure. This formula asserts no physical mechanism. It clarifies the metaphysical claim that divine and creaturely agency need not be zero-sum.

Divine action must nevertheless satisfy three requirements. It must be **efficacious**, or it becomes an interpretive label on a causally closed history. It must respect **created integrity**, rather than routinely destroying natural regularity or creaturely responsibility. And it must be **discernible without becoming mechanically coercive**: available to recognition through event, encounter, pattern, or fruit, yet not reducible to a technology for compelling God or compelling belief.

## Epistemic room and rational disagreement

Kenotic restraint applies not only to physical power but also to disclosure. A God committed to non-dominating relationship has reason not to overwhelm finite knowers with a manifestation that makes resistance to the proposition *God exists* rationally impossible. If a philosophical or empirical demonstration eliminated every reasonable alternative, then intellectually responsible people with access to it would be rationally compelled to assent. Philosophy would, in that limited sense, have proved God.

Such compulsion would not force love, trust, or worship. Knowledge that another person exists does not determine one's relationship to that person, and rational obligation is not identical to violent coercion. Nevertheless, irresistible proof would occupy the epistemic field in a way analogous to exhaustive causal control: it would remove reasonable nonbelief as a live position. If God's purpose includes a relationship in which recognition can be sought, interpreted, trusted, refused, and revisited without cognitive domination, then some **epistemic remainder** is to be expected:

\[
\text{evidence sufficient for responsible trust}
\;<\;
\text{evidence that rationally compels universal assent}.
\]

The remainder is not sheer obscurity. Total concealment would make relationship unavailable, while total evidential saturation would make sincere disagreement unavailable. Kenotic disclosure therefore occupies a narrow region between absence and overwhelm: enough intelligibility for belief to be rational, enough underdetermination for disbelief also to remain rationally possible.

This claim must not be converted into an automatic explanation of hiddenness. Ambiguity is not positive evidence for God merely because kenosis can accommodate it. Nor does the value of rational disagreement show that every instance of nonresistant nonbelief is necessary or good. The proposal instead makes a defeasible prediction: if revelation is kenotic, the evidence should support responsible commitment without erasing reasonable disagreement. Evidence so weak that relationship is practically inaccessible, or hiddenness that falls arbitrarily upon sincere seekers, remains adverse evidence. The epistemic remainder preserves the possibility of rational freedom; it does not certify every actual distribution of evidence as optimal.

Within the Spin(2,3) and \(E_8\) research programme, this has a limited structural analogue. A mathematical arena may determine an admissible family without uniquely selecting the realized physical direction. A selector can operate without rewriting the carrier space. The theological idea of providence as a participatory selector is therefore suggestive, but it is not a derivation of theology from \(E_8\), a physical mechanism of providence, or evidence that the mathematical programme describes divine action. The analogy is valuable only while that boundary remains explicit.

## Formation rather than instantaneous correction

The purpose of divine action on this account is not merely to transmit correct propositions. It is to form agents capable of recognizing, choosing, and sustaining the good. Information and formation are not equivalent:

\[
\text{being told the answer}
\neq
\text{becoming capable of understanding and living it}.
\]

The analogy with raising children is illuminating when used carefully. Good formation involves stable boundaries, consequences calibrated to capacity, protection from catastrophic harm, gradually enlarged responsibility, and love that survives resistance. Its end is not permanent dependence but mature participation.

A developmental sequence can be represented as

\[
\text{command}
\rightarrow
\text{boundary}
\rightarrow
\text{example}
\rightarrow
\text{internalized discernment}
\rightarrow
\text{mature cooperation}.
\]

Some biblical narratives can be read through this lens. The binding of Isaac in Genesis 22 can function canonically as an arrest and transformation of sacrificial logic: faithfulness does not finally require possession of the child to culminate in the child's destruction. Because the narrative begins with a divine command to bind Isaac, it cannot be treated as an uncomplicated moral lesson or as historical proof that one episode ended child sacrifice. Its kenotic significance lies in the dramatic refusal of the child's death and the opening of a different religious possibility.

First Samuel 8 provides an especially clear picture of consequential agency. Israel requests a king amid institutional failure: Samuel is old and his sons are corrupt. God instructs Samuel to warn the people what concentrated royal power will do. The king will take sons, daughters, fields, produce, servants, and flocks, until the people themselves become servants. Yet after warning them, God tells Samuel to listen to their voice. The sequence is

\[
\text{interpretation}
\rightarrow
\text{warning}
\rightarrow
\text{permission}
\rightarrow
\text{consequence}
\rightarrow
\text{continued accompaniment}.
\]

God neither conceals the institutional dynamics nor forcibly cancels the people's decision. Nor does permission become endorsement. Prophetic criticism, judgment of kings, and the later transformation of kingship remain possible within the compromised institution Israel has chosen.

The child analogy also imposes a severe limit. A good parent may permit manageable consequences for growth but does not allow one child to torture another indefinitely so that the family can learn. Societies are not single children, and the people paying for an institution's moral education are often not those exercising the decisive power. Formation therefore cannot justify treating victims as educational material. Accommodation is not endorsement; developmental benefit must be proportionate; intervention thresholds must exist; and those who bear the cost require protection, justice, and restoration.

## Intervention, boundaries, and anti-predation

Non-intervention is not intrinsically kenotic. Unrestricted action by one creature can destroy the agency of another. A theology of non-domination therefore requires an anti-predation principle:

> **Restraint ceases to protect agency when it predictably permits one agent to eliminate the effective agency of another.**

Restricting a predator may increase rather than diminish freedom overall. A boundary limits one person's available actions precisely to preserve the shared field in which many persons can act. Unconditional love does not mean unconditional access or exemption from consequences.

For a possible intervention \(i\), distinguish its first-, second-, and third-order effects:

- \(H_1(i)\): immediate harms prevented or produced;
- \(H_2(i)\): altered incentives, retaliation, dependency, displacement, and future choices;
- \(H_3(i)\): institutions, norms, capacities, precedents, and long-run attractors created.

An intervention may stop immediate harm while strengthening the structure that reproduces it. Aid may relieve suffering while preserving an exploitative institution. Punishment may incapacitate an offender while normalizing retaliation. Conversely, refusal to intervene may preserve one agent's nominal choice while permitting that agent to erase the practical freedom of many others.

A kenotically admissible intervention should therefore satisfy the following conditions:

1. **Necessity:** a serious threat to life, agency, or relational possibility exists.
2. **Effectiveness:** the action has a reasonable prospect of addressing that threat.
3. **Proportionality:** it uses no more domination than protection requires.
4. **Lawful integrity:** it preserves intelligibility and causal regularity where possible.
5. **Agency restoration:** it protects or restores agency rather than producing permanent dependency.
6. **Structural awareness:** it considers higher-order effects, not merely the immediate symptom.
7. **Least displacement:** no comparably effective and less agency-displacing action is available.
8. **Restorative orientation:** justified restraint leaves open repentance, repair, and safe reintegration where possible.

These criteria govern divine and human action analogically, not identically. God and finite agents differ radically in knowledge and capability. Nevertheless, the criteria prevent *kenosis* from becoming a synonym for either inactivity or permissiveness.

## Shared and differentiated responsibility

If creaturely agency is real, human beings are not merely observers waiting for divine correction. They are entrusted participants with power to prevent harm, establish boundaries, repair relationships, reform institutions, and protect those whose agency is being destroyed. The question "Why did God allow this?" is sometimes incomplete until it is joined by four others:

\[
\text{Who knew? Who could act? Who benefited? Who remained silent?}
\]

Invoking divine sovereignty can become an abrogation of delegated human responsibility. If God performed every act of justice that human beings could perform, human moral agency would become largely ceremonial.

Responsibility must nevertheless be differentiated rather than assigned vaguely to "humanity." For an agent \(x\) and event \(e\), write schematically

\[
\rho_x(e)
=
f\!\left(K_x,P_x,O_x,V_x,C_x\right),
\]

where \(K_x\) is relevant knowledge, \(P_x\) effective power, \(O_x\) opportunity, \(V_x\) role-based obligation, and \(C_x\) coercion or incapacity. Responsibility normally increases with knowledge, power, opportunity, and obligation, and decreases with coercion and incapacity. The predator, beneficiary, institutional enabler, resistant witness, and victim do not bear equal responsibility.

Divine action may frequently be mediated through human action:

\[
A_{\mathrm{divine}}
\rightarrow
\text{perception, conscience, courage, and coordination}
\rightarrow
A_{\mathrm{human}}
\rightarrow
\text{historical change}.
\]

This is a theological interpretation, not an automatic inference from every benevolent action to God. It shows how divine and human agency may cooperate without occupying mutually exclusive causal spaces.

The history of slavery illustrates both the promise and danger of this claim. Human equality, liberation, neighbour-love, testimony by enslaved people, organized resistance, political struggle, institutional reform, and changing economic conditions contributed to abolition. Christian language and institutions were used both to oppose slavery and to defend it. A kenotic account cannot credit abolition to God while assigning centuries of accommodation entirely to human failure. It must absorb the delay, the complicity, and the fact that the costs of moral formation were imposed upon those with the least power. Divine action through history does not relieve participants of responsibility; it intensifies the question of whether they joined liberation or resisted it.

## Wrath as fidelity to consequential reality

Divine wrath need not be understood as an uncontrolled divine emotion or an arbitrary penalty externally attached to an act. Actions alter the state in which later action occurs. This is true physically, psychologically, relationally, and institutionally:

\[
S_{t+1}=F(S_t,A_t,\mathcal L).
\]

Betrayal reduces trust and raises the cost of future cooperation. Domination produces concealment, resistance, and institutional fragility. Repeated violence normalizes violence and trains new perpetrators. Concentrated power attracts self-protective behaviour. Forgiveness may interrupt retaliation without instantly restoring trust. These dynamics exhibit feedback, thresholds, path dependence, compounding effects, and hysteresis: history remains active after the initiating event has passed.

Sin can therefore be described not only as rule-breaking but as participation in dynamics that deform both the agent and the shared world:

\[
\text{destructive act}
\rightarrow
\text{damaged relation}
\rightarrow
\text{narrower future possibilities}
\rightarrow
\text{further destructive acts}.
\]

On this account, wrath is God's fidelity to the truth of acts. God does not call domination love, make betrayal consequence-free by declaration, or preserve oppressive systems indefinitely from their contradictions. Judgment may include truthful exposure, the removal of restraint from a persistently chosen trajectory, institutional collapse, or direct corrective action.

The identification must remain qualified:

\[
\text{divine wrath}
\sim
\text{natural and relational consequence},
\qquad
\text{not}
\qquad
\text{suffering}=\text{personal guilt}.
\]

Consequences propagate through networks and generations. Innocent people suffer from another person's violence, inherited institutions, natural processes, and sheer contingency. Suffering is not a reliable measure of guilt. Any theology that infers blame from misfortune confuses the existence of consequential order with the moral innocence of every outcome.

Grace likewise need not mean cancellation of causality. It can be understood as the introduction of a new causal possibility into a closed destructive loop:

\[
\text{destructive attractor}
\xrightarrow{\text{grace}}
\text{a costly path toward restoration}.
\]

Forgiveness does not make betrayal unhappen. Liberation does not erase enslavement. Resurrection does not pretend crucifixion was unreal. Grace opens a future that the damaged system could not generate from its existing momentum alone.

## Incarnation, cross, and resurrection

The lawful and developmental account explains why a world with agency requires stable consequences, why constant correction could undermine formation, and why divine action might ordinarily be non-dominating. It does not show that every particular instance of suffering was necessary. After these explanations have done all they can, a remainder persists:

\[
\text{freedom}+\text{law}+\text{formation}
\not\Rightarrow
\text{every suffering is justified}.
\]

That remainder is tragedy: real loss not presently shown to be necessary, proportionate, or good.

Incarnation is the bridge across this remainder. God does not merely explain suffering from beyond the world but enters the conditions under which creatures suffer. In the Christian narrative, God accepts embodiment, dependence, vulnerability, political violence, grief, abandonment, and death. The creator does not ask creatures to endure a condition from which the creator remains exempt.

Jesus is the fullest disclosure of kenotic power. Messianic authority is not exercised through retaliatory conquest or self-preservation at any cost. The cross presents power capable of retaliation yet governed by love that refuses domination:

\[
\text{capacity for retaliation}
+\text{love refusing retaliation}
=\text{kenotic sovereignty}.
\]

The resurrection is necessary to prevent this from becoming a theology that sanctifies victimhood. Cross without resurrection risks making goodness synonymous with submission to destruction. Resurrection without the cross risks restoring triumphal power. Together they claim that self-giving love is neither domination nor final defeat.

The Christian response to tragedy is thus not exhaustive explanation but presence, solidarity, judgment, and restoration:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{Creation} &: \text{God makes room for genuine otherness},\\
\text{Law} &: \text{God grants that otherness stable consequence},\\
\text{Kenosis} &: \text{God refuses to monopolize its history},\\
\text{Incarnation} &: \text{God enters its vulnerability},\\
\text{Cross} &: \text{God bears relational violence},\\
\text{Resurrection} &: \text{God refuses tragedy final authority}.
\end{aligned}
\]

Participation does not by itself answer why God failed to prevent a particular horror. Nor does later restoration make the horror instrumentally good. The defensible claim is narrower:

\[
\text{redemption of suffering}
\neq
\text{retroactive justification of suffering}.
\]

God may bring good from evil without making evil necessary or good.

## The danger of a self-sealing theology

The kenotic account becomes empty if every possible observation confirms it. If success is called providence, failure hiddenness, harm formation, non-intervention respect for freedom, and intervention mercy, no state of the world can count against the theory.

The epistemic remainder is especially vulnerable to this error. It cannot mean that every absence of evidence confirms a God who prefers not to overwhelm. The argument is conditional: given a divine purpose of non-dominating relationship, conclusive public proof may carry a relational cost by eliminating reasonable nonbelief. It does not follow that greater clarity is always worse, that present evidence has the right distribution, or that a perfectly loving God would permit indefinitely inaccessible relationship. A world containing both rational belief and rational disagreement fits the model; it does not prove the model.

The phrase *kenotically optimal* is especially dangerous. To establish genuine optimality one would need to specify the available divine policies, the goods being optimized, the weights assigned to competing goods, and the consequences of every alternative. Without those elements, optimality is not a conclusion but a placeholder for an unknown justification.

The burden of proof differs between three claims:

1. **Logical defence:** God and suffering are not demonstrably contradictory because God might possess morally sufficient reasons.
2. **Theodicy:** identifiable considerations such as law, agency, formation, and non-domination make some divine restraint intelligible.
3. **Optimality:** this particular permission was the best available divine policy.

A possible unknown reason may block a strict deduction of inconsistency. It does not establish that such a reason actually exists, much less that every event belongs to the best possible history:

\[
\neg\operatorname{Proved}(\neg\exists R)
\not\Rightarrow
\exists R.
\]

It is therefore better to ask whether a policy is **kenotically non-dominated** than whether it is uniquely optimal. A history is challenged when a critic identifies an apparently available intervention \(i'\) that would substantially reduce grave harm without comparably damaging intelligibility, agency, formation, relational freedom, or the agency of others.

If, relative to the actual history \(h\),

\[
H(i')<H(h)
\]

while

\[
I(i')\ge I(h),\quad
A(i')\ge A(h),\quad
F(i')\ge F(h),\quad
R(i')\ge R(h),
\]

then the model owes an account of the good sacrificed by \(i'\). The critic need not reconstruct God's complete decision space. A plausible dominating alternative is enough to create an evidential burden. Replying that an unknown cost must exist preserves logical possibility but reduces explanatory force.

This yields the governing epistemic rule:

\[
\text{unexplained}\neq\text{incompatible},
\qquad
\text{but also}
\qquad
\text{unexplained}\neq\text{optimally permitted}.
\]

## Conditions of philosophical accountability

The kenotic model remains substantive only if it accepts the following restrictions:

1. Kenosis cannot explain intervention and non-intervention without independent criteria.
2. Appeals to unknown reasons preserve possibility but do not count as positive evidence.
3. Favourable and adverse evidence must be treated symmetrically.
4. The value of rational disagreement cannot make every degree or distribution of divine hiddenness optimal.
5. Irresistible proof would compel assent to God's existence, not love, trust, or worship; the distinction limits the hiddenness argument.
6. Nonresistant nonbelief, apparently gratuitous suffering, and destructive religious outcomes remain adverse evidence.
7. Human responsibility explains only harms within meaningful human power; it does not answer suffering beyond human capacity.
8. Formation cannot justify using victims merely for another person's development.
9. Restoration cannot make present suffering morally irrelevant.
10. Divine solidarity cannot replace preventable rescue without further argument.
11. No individual instance of suffering may be inferred to express personal guilt merely because consequences are real.
12. The theory must permit some conceivable worlds and patterns of evidence to count strongly against it.

These restrictions do not produce a mechanical test of God. They prevent the theory from converting every outcome into confirmation.

## The constructive argument

The complete argument can now be stated:

1. Genuine created otherness requires some degree of created causal integrity.
2. Causal integrity requires stable regularities and actions with real consequences.
3. Stable regularity makes intelligibility, responsibility, and cumulative formation possible.
4. Consequential agency entails the possibility of harmful choice and tragic outcomes.
5. Perfect goodness gives God reason not to dominate created agency merely to secure preferred compliance.
6. The same non-dominating purpose gives God reason not to monopolize the evidential field by making reasonable nonbelief impossible.
7. Kenotic disclosure should therefore make responsible belief possible without making assent to God's existence rationally irresistible.
8. Perfect goodness also gives God reason to protect creatures against agency-destroying predation.
9. Divine action should therefore be efficacious, proportionate, non-dominating, and directed toward restoring the shared field of agency.
10. Creatures with relevant knowledge and power inherit genuine responsibility to resist predation and repair harm.
11. Natural, relational, and institutional consequences explain part of what religious language calls wrath, but suffering does not imply guilt.
12. Freedom, law, formation, and epistemic openness do not presently justify every instance of suffering or hiddenness.
13. Suffering not presently shown to be justified remains tragedy and adverse evidence rather than being renamed a hidden good.
14. Incarnation constitutes divine participation in tragedy; the cross discloses serving power; resurrection grounds hope for restoration.
15. Kenotic theism is therefore a coherent and potentially explanatory account of divine restraint and action, but it does not prove that every actual non-intervention or degree of hiddenness was optimal.

The argument does not solve the problem of evil by eliminating its evidential force. It instead identifies the conditions under which divine restraint may be morally intelligible, the conditions under which intervention becomes obligatory, the responsibility transferred to created agents, and the tragedy that remains when explanation reaches its limit.

## Conclusion

The tri-omni God is not best conceived as an unlimited causal or epistemic competitor who secures goodness by controlling every event or compelling every rational conclusion. Omnipotence governed by omnibenevolence takes kenotic form: it establishes a stable and intelligible world, makes room for real agency and reasonable disagreement, refuses domination, sets boundaries against predation, participates within history, and works toward restoration without denying consequence.

This vision distributes rather than evades responsibility. God is responsible for creating and sustaining the arena and cannot be exempted from questions about its costs. Human beings are responsible according to their knowledge, power, opportunity, and role, and cannot assign to God the wrongs they possess the means to resist. Victims are not responsible for providing the suffering through which perpetrators or societies mature.

The irreducible remainder is tragedy. Christianity's distinctive response is not that every tragedy can be demonstrated to have been necessary. It is that God crosses the distance between creator and creature, enters the world's suffering, bears its violence, and refuses to grant death the final word.

The kenotic claim can therefore be expressed compactly:

> God creates a lawful but open world: ordered enough for truth, identity, consequential agency, and responsible belief; open enough for novelty, relationship, divine participation, and reasonable disagreement. God's power serves rather than monopolizes either causality or evidence. God's restraint preserves agency but is bounded by opposition to predation. Human beings receive real responsibility for one another. Where freedom, law, formation, and epistemic openness do not explain suffering or hiddenness, the unexplained remains adverse evidence rather than proof of secret optimality. In incarnation God enters the tragic remainder, in the cross God bears it, and in resurrection God promises that it will not be final.
