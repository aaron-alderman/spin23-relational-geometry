# From “this sounds stupid” to an inspectable structure

**Date:** 19 October 2026  
**Status:** first applied case; deliberately independent of the earlier research programme

## Why this is the correct starting point

The previous work tried to map illegible knowledge by inventing dimensions and assigning labels. That reverses the order of discovery. A map should be induced from occasions on which somebody knows that something is wrong, right, dangerous, trustworthy, elegant or alive **before** they can fully state why.

The immediately preceding exchange supplies a real case.

The initial judgment was:

> “You are sounding stupid.”

That was not initially a theory. It was a high-confidence recognition. The subsequent explanation made part of its structure legible:

- material from an earlier context had been imported into a different inquiry;
- reuse of a chat was mistaken for continuity of subject;
- the response engaged with its own accumulated interpretation rather than the present speaker;
- an elegant synthesis concealed a basic relevance failure.

This is exactly the kind of transition we need to study:

```text
felt mismatch
    ↓
interruption
    ↓
contrast and correction
    ↓
candidate cues
    ↓
rule tested on new cases
```

## 1. The raw event

One participant was discussing illegible human knowledge. The other produced a polished explanation that absorbed the discussion into an older, unrelated frame. The response was recognized as bad before a complete account of the failure was supplied. The conversation was stopped. Only then was the distinction made explicit: shared conversational history does not imply continuity of subject.

The stop itself is evidence. The judgment changed action before it had become a full explanation.

## 2. What the judgment discriminated

“Sounding stupid” did not merely mean “containing a false proposition.” It detected a compound failure:

| Detected feature | What occurred | What a good response would have done |
|---|---|---|
| relevance failure | answered a nearby historical topic | answered the presently intended topic |
| frame capture | forced new material into an existing frame | allowed a new local frame to form |
| false integration | treated conceptual smoothness as understanding | preserved discontinuity where it mattered |
| speaker displacement | privileged the system's interpretation | updated from the speaker's correction |
| fluency camouflage | coherent prose made the error harder to see | used clarity in service of contact |

The judgment therefore appears to track **contact**, not just correctness. A response can be coherent and still fail to make contact with what another person is doing.

## 3. A first candidate structure

This case suggests that one part of illegible social understanding monitors several alignments at once:

1. **referential alignment** — are we speaking about the same thing?
2. **intentional alignment** — am I responding to what you are trying to do?
3. **scale alignment** — am I operating at the requested level of abstraction?
4. **temporal alignment** — does old context still govern this moment?
5. **consequence alignment** — does my response move the situation in the desired direction?

Call this provisional vector

\[
A=(a_r,a_i,a_s,a_t,a_c).
\]

This is not a universal personality model. It is a measurement proposal extracted from one event. Its value depends on whether it predicts judgments in fresh cases.

The recognition may behave less like an average than a weakest-link test:

\[
\operatorname{contact}(A)\approx \min(a_r,a_i,a_s,a_t,a_c).
\]

A response can be impressive on four dimensions and still feel completely wrong because one necessary alignment has collapsed. This is a testable account of why fluent but irrelevant speech can feel worse than a plainly incomplete answer.

## 4. What remains illegible

The explanation does not yet tell us:

- which phrase first triggered the judgment;
- whether the error accumulated or crossed a sharp threshold;
- whether tone, length or confidence intensified it;
- which alternate response would have felt genuinely engaged;
- whether another observer would detect the failure at the same moment.

Those are the next measurements, not philosophical loose ends.

## 5. Elicitation by reconstruction

Do not begin with “Why did you feel that?” That invites a polished retrospective theory.

### Preserve

Record what happened immediately before the judgment, its first unedited expression, the action it caused, confidence at the time, and any noticed bodily or attentional change.

### Locate the boundary

Replay or reconstruct the episode in small steps:

- Where is the first moment it begins to go wrong?
- Which previous moment still feels acceptable?
- What is the smallest change that repairs it?
- What change makes it worse while preserving surface meaning?

### Construct contrasts

Create four nearby cases:

1. same words, different situation;
2. different words, same apparent intention;
3. polished but misaligned;
4. clumsy but genuinely engaged.

Ask for forced comparisons before reasons. The choices are data even when the reasons remain incomplete.

### Derive rival rules

Only after the contrasts should an analyst propose features. Each feature must point to an observed difference. Multiple rival rules remain live.

### Predict

Apply each candidate rule to unseen cases before showing them to the original judge. A feature map that redescribes its training examples has not made knowledge legible.

## 6. Rival explanations

| Hypothesis | Prediction |
|---|---|
| contextual contamination | removing references to the prior subject largely repairs the response |
| abstraction mismatch | a topic-pure answer still fails if it remains abstract rather than applied |
| agency mismatch | the main failure is acting on an interpretation instead of following the speaker's move |
| verbosity/confidence mismatch | a shorter, tentative version of the same interpretation is judged less badly |

A real exercise varies these independently. The goal is not a satisfying label but discovery of which perturbations change the judgment.

## 7. Degrees of legibility

| Level | Achievement |
|---|---|
| 0 | “This is wrong.” |
| 1 | the person identifies a contrasting case |
| 2 | controlled edits move the boundary |
| 3 | a compact feature set predicts fresh judgments |
| 4 | another person learns the discrimination faster using the map |
| 5 | the map transfers to a different setting without erasing important differences |

An eloquent explanation may reach only level 1. A crude training set that reliably teaches discrimination may reach level 4. Legibility is assessed by transfer and intervention, not prose quality.

## 8. The LLM's role

The LLM should serve as an experimental instrument:

- preserve the first expression without cleaning it up;
- generate minimal contrast cases;
- vary one feature at a time;
- propose competing explanations;
- predict classifications on held-out examples;
- record exceptions rather than smoothing them away;
- build a teaching sequence from easy contrasts to difficult near misses.

The human remains the source of the discrimination and the judge of whether contact has been preserved. The model earns confidence only by predicting and teaching distinctions it was not explicitly given.

## 9. First result

This case does not reveal the universal structure of illegible knowledge. It establishes a workable unit of inquiry:

\[
\boxed{
\text{episode}+\text{pre-explanatory judgment}+\text{action}
+\text{minimal contrasts}+\text{held-out prediction}
}
\]

It also yields one substantive candidate: people may possess a fine-grained, partly tacit sense of conversational contact integrating reference, intention, scale, temporal context and consequence. It can fail catastrophically when any necessary alignment is lost.

That is modest, but it is an actual piece of illegible structure made partially legible from a real event.
