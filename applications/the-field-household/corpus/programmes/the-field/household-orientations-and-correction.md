# Household orientations and revisable commitments

**Date:** 7 November 2026

**Status:** published corpus reading route and executable synthetic case; uncalibrated

**Scope:** human relational applications of orientation and correction; no universal household prescription or physical identification

Two people can welcome quite different household arrangements. One couple may value specialization, another equal participation, another flexibility around current capacities, and another taking turns to support demanding projects. Their reasons can differ even when their daily work looks similar. Their principles can be untidy while their coordination remains satisfying.

The harder question comes when something changes. Illness may make the former workload impossible without changing anyone's values. A new aspiration may change what one person wants their life to become. Additional caregiving can introduce obligations neither partner previously anticipated. A workable arrangement must then confront a different situation, and perhaps a disagreement about what the relationship means.

This case keeps three questions separate: **what each person values, what the arrangement produces, and how either person can seek to change it.** None answers the others automatically. A welcome outcome does not establish a universal rule. An available review does not guarantee agreement. Disagreement does not establish that either participant's values are defective.

Open the [household explorer](../../../preview/the-field/household-explorer-v2.html) to compare four orientations, five changes in circumstances and five patterns of response and follow-through. It is an inspectable model of declared assumptions, not a predictor of actual marriages. The complete [qualitative case and 66 attributed atomic claims](household-orientations-v1.md) preserve the motivating public argument without promoting its empirical claims to findings.

## What symmetry reveals and what it cannot settle

Start with a simple transformation: exchange A and B, carrying each person's capacities, goals, wage, experience history and learning rates with them. Complement the assigned task shares. This is a relabeling test, not a demand that the two people have identical circumstances or do identical amounts of work.

The [reproducible symmetry audit](../../../verification/verify/verify_the_field_household_symmetry_v1.mjs) finds that local allocation arithmetic and the willingness update do not depend on a sex label or participant name. But the entire simulation has additional conventions: the period's request agenda starts with a named participant, and some ties favor the smaller A share. Keeping those conventions fixed while exchanging people is capable of changing the trajectory.

The [retained audit receipt](../../../evidence/data/the-field/household-symmetry-v1-receipt.json) records 72 exchange-equivalent cases and 28 order-sensitive cases among the one hundred authored combinations, at a numerical tolerance of 1e-8. Its first counterexample is the roles/values/incomplete-implementation case at period 8: a different requester is selected after exchange, and willingness diverges even while the assigned shares still coincide. These counts describe this finite design, not percentages of real households.

This result is useful because it limits the model's authority. A rule can treat participants alike locally while its agenda still privileges a position in a sequence. A more complete symmetry would need to transform or replace those agenda conventions as well. That remains a testable extension rather than a symmetry claimed here. The executable model is retained unchanged so the counterexample stays reproducible.

Role exchange also does not prove moral equivalence. Exchanging names while leaving someone's dependence, health or history behind is a different intervention. The orientation manuscript's [discussion of transformations](../../../../../corpus/programmes/orientation/orientation_architecture.md#18-moral-truth-as-invariance-across-transformations) explicitly keeps those differences visible. Symmetry can expose an unexplained privilege; it cannot determine all the goods a relationship ought to pursue.

## Requests and commitments change the future

The [adaptive model](household-dynamics-v2.md) makes each participant's willingness to renegotiate depend on directed experience. A respectful response can support willingness even when the requested remedy is refused. Dismissal can reduce it. Actual completion of agreed work can reinforce it; incomplete implementation can reduce it. Capacity shortfalls remain distinct and are neutral under the default update rule.

An agreement is not marked kept just because it was signed. Work is executed under explicit capacity constraints, unfinished work remains visible and every willingness update names the event supporting it. A new mutually accepted allocation supersedes the old one rather than retrospectively declaring it breached. Changed practice can support recovery without resetting willingness or erasing earlier outcomes. Trust and moral standing are not inferred from these numbers.

The model retains immediate work and money consequences, changes in the continuing arrangement, and future possibilities shaped by willingness, reserves and unfinished tasks. It therefore exposes paths that a final happiness claim or a single household average could conceal. It also leaves out much of relationship life, including actual testimony, affection, work quality, coercion, outside support and the practical costs of separation.

Read the [qualitative comparisons](household-orientations-v1.md#comparing-four-household-orientations-through-change) for alternative orientations and changes in values or circumstances. Use the [fixed-willingness example](household-dynamics-v1.md) as a comparator, then inspect the [adaptive successor](household-dynamics-v2.md) and its [five experience presets](../../../evidence/data/the-field/household-orientations-v2.json). The [symmetry receipt](../../../evidence/data/the-field/household-symmetry-v1-receipt.json) qualifies any claim that those trajectories are independent of participant ordering.

The example has no numerical objective for finding the best marriage. Its adjustable rates are synthetic, its comparison households are fictional, and its internally consistent trajectories do not validate the tweet's predictions. Its contribution is a concrete way to investigate how different commitments can work, become infeasible, be revised or remain in conflict, while keeping each participant and the conditions of future correction visible.
